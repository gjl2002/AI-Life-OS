# Theory Library

Use this as the built-in method source for user-avatar work. Do not search the user's workspace for a separate theory library unless the user explicitly names one as a source.

Theory is for analysis method, not evidence. Never use theory to prove what the user's actual customers think.

## Decision Order

Analyze in this order:
1. What did real users say or do?
2. What repeated pattern appears across materials?
3. What business context explains why the pattern matters?
4. Which theory helps name the pattern?
5. What constraints or questions should be handed to positioning, product, content, sales, or interviews?

If there is no real user material, output a hypothesis avatar and validation plan.

## Persona

Use persona thinking to make research patterns memorable and decision-useful. A persona is not a decorative fictional character; it is a representative model of repeated user goals, behaviors, contexts, and constraints.

Use persona for:
- making the user type easy to remember
- aligning content, product, and sales decisions
- describing the user's lived context
- naming the user in language the business can reuse

Avoid persona theater:
- fake names with no evidence
- demographic stereotypes
- "25-35, first-tier cities, self-growth" as the main insight
- colorful biographies that do not guide decisions

## Buyer Persona

Use buyer persona thinking to analyze purchase logic. For every commercial avatar, answer:
- Why would this person buy?
- Why would this person not buy?
- What event creates urgency?
- What result feels worth paying for?
- What risk do they fear after paying?
- What proof or relationship builds trust?
- What competing option are they comparing against?
- What words do they use before purchase?

For knowledge products, buyer persona is often more important than demographic persona. The key is not "who they are" but "what makes them finally act".

## User Persona vs Buyer Persona

Separate:
- User persona: how the person uses the product, system, service, course, or template after purchase.
- Buyer persona: why the person pays, delays, refuses, trusts, or compares.

For knowledge products, a person may be a good learner/user but a weak buyer, or a strong buyer but poor-fit user. Mark this distinction when visible.

## Jobs To Be Done

Use JTBD to identify the task the user is hiring the product to perform.

Look for:
- trigger situation
- desired progress
- functional job
- emotional job
- social job
- competing solutions
- forces pushing change
- forces blocking change

Use this sentence pattern:

```text
When [trigger/context], this user wants to [desired progress], so they can [functional/emotional/social outcome].
```

Prefer JTBD over demographic labels when explaining why the user cares.

## Four Forces of Progress

For purchase and adoption decisions, look for:
- Push of the current situation: pain, chaos, dissatisfaction, failed attempts.
- Pull of the new solution: desired outcome, identity, promise, convenience.
- Anxiety of the new solution: price, trust, complexity, fear of not using it.
- Habit of the present: existing tools, procrastination, familiar workflows.

Use these forces to explain why someone buys now, delays, or refuses.

## User Interview Discipline

Use interview discipline to avoid false positives:
- ask about past behavior, not imaginary future behavior
- ask for concrete scenes and recent examples
- ask what they tried before
- ask what they paid with money, time, attention, or reputation
- ask what almost stopped them
- ask what changed right before they acted
- treat compliments as weak evidence
- treat repeated concrete behavior as strong evidence

Good follow-up questions:
- "Can you show me the last time this happened?"
- "What did you try before this?"
- "What made you look for a solution now?"
- "What were you afraid would happen after paying?"
- "What would make you say this was worth it?"

## Thematic Analysis

Use thematic analysis to transform raw qualitative data into patterns:
1. collect raw notes and quotes
2. tag meaningful statements
3. cluster related tags
4. name themes in user language
5. separate frequency from intensity
6. connect themes to business decisions

Theme naming rules:
- Prefer the user's words over abstract labels.
- Combine similar words only after preserving original quotes.
- Separate a repeated weak theme from a rare but commercially important theme.
- Do not let one dramatic quote dominate unless it matches business context or other signals.

## Evidence Triangulation

Treat an insight as stronger when it appears across multiple source types:
- interview transcript
- private chat or consultation
- purchase/revenue record
- comment or public discussion
- delivery feedback
- product or content behavior

When sources disagree, state the tension instead of forcing one answer.

## Positioning

Use positioning to connect avatars to business strategy:
- who this product is really for
- which alternatives users compare against
- what value is most differentiated
- which users are poor fit
- which words make the offer legible
- which pain should be foregrounded

Do not let positioning override user evidence. Let it interpret why the evidence matters for this business.

## Offer Analysis

Use offer analysis to translate avatar insights into packaging and sales implications.

Check:
- promise: what concrete progress is being sold?
- audience: who is this especially for?
- mechanism: why can this work when other attempts failed?
- proof: what evidence makes the claim believable?
- risk reversal: what reduces anxiety?
- format: course, service, template, system, community, consultation
- price logic: what outcome justifies the price?
- delivery fit: can this user actually complete or use it?

For each avatar, identify whether the offer should emphasize:
- clarity
- speed
- companionship
- accountability
- system design
- personal diagnosis
- done-for-you support
- proof and case studies

## Sales Objection Analysis

Classify objections:
- price: "too expensive"
- trust: "will this really work?"
- self-doubt: "I probably won't use it"
- complexity: "I don't have energy to learn this"
- timing: "not now"
- relevance: "is this for me?"
- past failure: "I bought similar things and abandoned them"
- privacy or exposure: "I don't want to share too much"

Convert each objection into:
- evidence needed
- sales-page response
- product-design response
- interview follow-up question

## Content And Sales Handoff

Extract evidence-backed expression constraints for downstream work:

- which pain, scene, mechanism, proof, identity, or objection is actually present;
- which user phrases should be preserved;
- which promises would overstate the evidence;
- which misunderstanding or pressure should be avoided.

Do not turn this research step into a full content plan, sales page, or sales SOP. Route execution to the corresponding Skill.

## Validation Design

When evidence is weak, design the smallest next validation step that could change the judgment. Possible starting points include:
- interview a small number of people from the suspected segment
- collect a focused batch of recent private chats or consultations
- ask non-buyers why they did not buy
- compare buyer and non-buyer language
- test one sales-page angle
- test one content topic based on the strongest pain

Validation questions should target the weakest part of the avatar:
- unclear trigger
- unclear willingness to pay
- unclear objection
- unclear target segment
- unclear product fit
- unclear content hook

## Output Discipline

Every avatar should separate:
- confirmed facts
- reasonable insights
- hypotheses to verify

If a statement cannot be tied to user evidence or business context, remove it or label it as a hypothesis.

The final user-avatar result may state downstream constraints, but it must not complete the full commercial positioning, product architecture, content plan, sales page, or sales process.
